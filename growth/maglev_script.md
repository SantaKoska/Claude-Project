# How a magnetic train works: two magnets that refuse to touch (Video 13, English)

**Our way:** small cause -> giant effect in the first breath (two magnets pushing apart lift a train and make it the fastest on Earth), then how (1934 patent, superconducting magnets, figure-eight coils, the track is the motor), a silence, the twist (the "wheelless" train has rubber wheels), payoff (603 km/h) and callback.
**Emotion:** wonder. **Share reason:** the wheels twist, and "the track is the engine" is the kind of fact people send to a friend.
**Hook card (0-3 s):** TWO MAGNETS. 603 KM/H.

## ElevenLabs paste block

```
Two magnets... pushing each other away.

That's all it took to lift a train into the air, and make it the fastest train on Earth.

How? Let's take one apart.

1934. A German engineer, Hermann Kemper, patents a train with no wheels.

Decades later, Japan builds its own. Under every car, superconducting magnets, cooled to minus two hundred and sixty-nine degrees.

In the walls of the track, figure-eight coils. As the magnets rush past, the coils push the train up ten centimetres... and hold it in the centre.

And there's no engine on board. The track is the motor. Its coils flip north and south, pulling the train from the front, pushing it from behind.

...

But here's the surprise. This train has wheels. Rubber tyres. Until it's fast enough, it can't float at all. It rolls, then lifts off, like a plane.

April 21st, 2015. Yamanashi, Japan. Six hundred and three kilometres an hour.

All from two magnets... that refuse to touch.
```

## Accuracy notes

- Lift-off speed is given as 150 km/h in some sources and 160 km/h in others, so the script says "until it's fast enough" instead of a number.
- "Like a plane" is an analogy for rolling then lifting off, not a claim about the tyre type.
- "Fastest train on Earth" is the rail speed record (603 km/h). It is a test record; the L0 is not in passenger service yet, so the script doesn't say people ride it at that speed.
- "Decades later, Japan builds its own": Japan's national railway began maglev research in 1962. Kemper's design led to Germany's Transrapid; Japan's SCMaglev uses a different method (superconducting magnets, repulsion), hence "its own".
- The train in the video is our own design inspired by long-nose maglevs, not a copy of JR Central's livery or logo.
